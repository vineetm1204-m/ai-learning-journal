# Day 47 — Weight initialization strategies: Xavier, He, random

> 📅 August 12, 2026

---

## 🧠 Concept Explanation

Weight initialization sets the starting values of a neural network's parameters before training begins. Good initialization keeps the variance of activations and gradients roughly constant across layers, preventing the vanishing or exploding gradient problems that stall learning. Xavier (Glorot) initialization assumes the activation function is roughly linear around zero (e.g., tanh, sigmoid) and scales weights so that the variance of outputs equals the variance of inputs: Var(W) = 2 / (n_in + n_out) for a normal distribution, or a uniform range of ±√(6/(n_in+n_out)). He initialization targets ReLU‑like activations, which zero out half the inputs; it therefore uses a larger variance, Var(W) = 2 / n_in (normal) or ±√(6/n_in) (uniform), to compensate for the lost signal. Simple random initialization (e.g., small fixed Gaussian) ignores layer width and activation shape, often leading to poor convergence. Modern libraries default to He for ReLU networks and Xavier for sigmoid/tanh, but practitioners should match the scheme to their specific activation and architecture.

---

## ✅ Key Points

- Xavier/Glorot keeps variance balanced for symmetric activations (tanh, sigmoid).
- He initialization doubles variance to offset ReLU's zero‑gate effect.
- Both methods scale with fan‑in (n_in) and/or fan‑out (n_out) to maintain signal magnitude.
- Uniform and normal variants exist; choice rarely matters if variance matches.
- Mismatched initialization can cause vanishing/exploding gradients and slow training.

---

## 🏗️ Diagram / Formula

```
Xavier (normal):   W ~ N(0, sqrt(2/(n_in + n_out)))
Xavier (uniform):  W ~ U(-sqrt(6/(n_in + n_out)), +sqrt(6/(n_in + n_out)))
He (normal):       W ~ N(0, sqrt(2/n_in))
He (uniform):      W ~ U(-sqrt(6/n_in), +sqrt(6/n_in))
```

---

## 💡 Real-World Analogy

Choosing an initialization is like setting the initial temperature of a chemical reaction: too cold and nothing happens (vanishing gradients), too hot and it blows up (exploding gradients); the right temperature lets the reaction proceed smoothly.

---

## ⚠️ Common Mistakes

- Using Xavier for ReLU networks, which under‑scales weights and slows convergence.
- Applying a single fixed std (e.g., 0.01) to all layers regardless of width.
- Forgetting to adjust initialization when swapping activation functions.
