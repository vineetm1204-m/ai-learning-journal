## 🧠 Concept Explanation

Neural Architecture Search (NAS) automates the design of neural network topologies, replacing manual trial‑and‑error with a systematic optimization process. A NAS system defines a search space of possible layers, connections, and hyper‑parameters, then employs a search strategy—such as reinforcement learning, evolutionary algorithms, or gradient‑based methods—to explore this space. A performance estimator (often a proxy like training for a few epochs or a weight‑sharing supernet) evaluates each candidate architecture, providing a reward signal that guides the search toward higher‑accuracy models. Early NAS approaches (e.g., Zoph & Le 2017) used a controller RNN trained with REINFORCE, which was computationally expensive, requiring thousands of GPU‑days. Later work introduced weight sharing (ENAS, DARTS) and one‑shot supernets, drastically reducing search cost by training a single over‑parameterized network once and sampling sub‑architectures. Modern NAS also incorporates multi‑objective criteria (latency, memory, energy) and hardware‑aware constraints, enabling deployment on edge devices. Despite progress, NAS remains challenging: search spaces can be huge, evaluation noise can mislead the controller, and the final architecture may overfit the proxy task. Practitioners often combine NAS with manual fine‑tuning and knowledge distillation to obtain robust, production‑ready models.

---

## ✅ Key Points

- NAS replaces hand‑crafted architectures with an automated search over a defined space.
- Search strategies include RL controllers, evolutionary algorithms, and differentiable (gradient‑based) methods.
- Weight‑sharing supernets (e.g., DARTS) reduce compute by training one over‑parameterized model.
- Multi‑objective NAS optimizes accuracy together with latency, memory, and energy constraints.
- Proxy evaluations and noise can cause overfitting; final models usually need fine‑tuning.

---

## ⚠️ Common Mistakes

- Using an excessively large search space without constraints, leading to prohibitive compute.
- Relying solely on low‑fidelity proxies (e.g., few epochs) and deploying the found architecture without full training.
- Ignoring hardware‑specific metrics (latency, memory) and ending up with a model that cannot run on the target device.
