## 🧠 Concept Explanation

Backpropagation is the algorithm that efficiently computes the gradient of a loss function with respect to every weight in a neural network. At its core lies the chain rule from calculus: the derivative of a composite function equals the product of the derivatives of its constituent functions. During the forward pass, each layer transforms its input, and the loss is evaluated at the end. The backward pass then walks this computation graph in reverse, multiplying local gradients (the derivative of each layer’s output with respect to its input and parameters) to accumulate the global gradient for each weight. Because the same intermediate values are reused many times, backpropagation avoids redundant calculations, yielding a cost that is roughly the same as a single forward pass. Intuitively, it tells each parameter how much it contributed to the final error, allowing gradient‑based optimizers to nudge the weights in the direction that reduces loss. This systematic credit assignment works for any differentiable architecture, from simple MLPs to deep convolutional or recurrent networks. The chain rule also explains why gradients can vanish or explode in very deep networks: repeated multiplication of numbers smaller or larger than one shrinks or blows up the signal. Modern tricks such as residual connections, batch normalization, and careful initialization keep the gradient flow healthy, making training of hundreds of layers feasible.

---

## ✅ Key Points

- Backprop applies the chain rule to a computational graph.
- Forward pass stores intermediate activations for reuse.
- Backward pass multiplies local gradients to get global gradients.
- Complexity is roughly one extra forward pass.
- Gradient health depends on architecture choices like residuals and normalization.

## ⚠️ Common Mistakes

- Forgetting to detach or stop‑gradient when computing auxiliary losses.
- Assuming the chain rule works the same for non‑differentiable operations (e.g., hard max).
- Neglecting to scale gradients, leading to vanishing or exploding updates in deep nets.
