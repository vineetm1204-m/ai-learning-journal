## 🧠 Concept Explanation

Neural Architecture Search (NAS) automates the design of neural network topologies instead of relying on human intuition. It treats architecture selection as an optimization problem: a search space defines all possible layer types, connections, and hyper‑parameters; a search strategy (e.g., reinforcement learning, evolutionary algorithms, gradient‑based methods) proposes candidate architectures; and a performance estimator (often a proxy like training for a few epochs or a weight‑sharing supernet) evaluates them. The goal is to discover architectures that maximize validation accuracy while respecting constraints such as latency, model size, or energy consumption. Early NAS methods required thousands of GPU‑days because each candidate was trained from scratch. Modern techniques like weight sharing (ENAS, DARTS) or progressive shrinking drastically reduce cost by training a single over‑parameterized supernet once and sampling sub‑networks. NAS has produced state‑of‑the‑art models (e.g., EfficientNet, MobileNetV3) that outperform hand‑crafted counterparts on image classification, detection, and even NLP tasks. However, the search space design, evaluation fidelity, and computational budget remain critical choices that determine whether NAS yields practical gains or merely academic curiosities.

---

## ✅ Key Points

- NAS frames architecture design as a search/optimization problem.
- Three core components: search space, search strategy, performance estimator.
- Weight‑sharing supernets (e.g., DARTS) cut search cost from thousands to hundreds of GPU‑hours.
- Discovered architectures often surpass manual designs on accuracy/efficiency trade‑offs.
- Practical deployment requires careful constraint handling (latency, memory, hardware).

---

## ⚠️ Common Mistakes

- Using an overly large search space without constraints, leading to prohibitive compute.
- Relying solely on low‑fidelity proxies (e.g., few epochs) and ignoring final training dynamics.
- Neglecting hardware‑aware metrics (latency, memory) so the found model cannot run on target devices.
