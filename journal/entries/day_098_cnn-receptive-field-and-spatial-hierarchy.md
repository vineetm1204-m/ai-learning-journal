# Day 98 — CNN receptive field and spatial hierarchy

## 🧠 Concept Explanation

Convolutional neural networks build a spatial hierarchy by stacking layers, each of which aggregates information from a local neighborhood of the previous layer. The receptive field of a unit is the region in the input image that influences its activation. In the first layer a 3×3 kernel sees a 3×3 patch; after a second 3×3 layer the effective receptive field grows to 5×5 because each unit in the second layer combines a 3×3 region of the first‑layer outputs, which themselves each cover 3×3 input pixels. Stride and pooling further enlarge the field without adding parameters. Deeper layers therefore capture larger, more abstract patterns such as object parts or whole objects, while early layers detect edges and textures. Understanding the receptive field helps designers choose kernel sizes, depths, and dilation rates to match the scale of the target features. It also explains why very deep networks can model long‑range dependencies despite using only small kernels. Modern architectures often employ dilated convolutions or feature‑pyramid modules to explicitly control the receptive field size and maintain resolution for dense prediction tasks. Practitioners can compute the exact receptive field analytically using the formula that accumulates kernel size, stride, and dilation across layers, enabling precise architecture tuning.

---

## ✅ Key Points

- Receptive field defines the input region that influences a neuron's activation.
- Stacking convolutional layers expands the receptive field multiplicatively.
- Stride, pooling, and dilation increase the field without adding parameters.
- Deeper layers capture larger, more abstract patterns (object parts, whole objects).
- Analytic calculation of receptive field guides kernel, depth, and dilation choices.

## 🏗️ Diagram / Formula

```
Input (3x3) -> Conv3x3 (RF 3x3)\n-> Conv3x3 (RF 5x5)\n-> Pool2x2 (RF ~10x10)
```

## 💡 Real-World Analogy

Like a photographer zooming out: early layers see fine details (pixels), later layers see the whole scene.

---

## ⚠️ Common Mistakes

- Assuming receptive field equals the kernel size of the current layer only.
- Ignoring stride and dilation when estimating the effective receptive field.
- Believing deeper networks are always better, neglecting resolution loss and over‑receptive fields.
