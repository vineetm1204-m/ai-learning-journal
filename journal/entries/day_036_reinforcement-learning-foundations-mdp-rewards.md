## 🧠 Concept Explanation

Reinforcement learning formalizes how an agent learns to act in an environment by interacting with it. At the core lies the Markov Decision Process (MDP), a tuple (S, A, P, R, γ) where S is the set of states, A the set of actions, P the transition probability P(s'|s,a), R the reward function R(s,a,s'), and γ the discount factor. The agent follows a policy π(a|s) that maps states to a distribution over actions. The goal is to find a policy that maximizes the expected cumulative discounted reward, called the return G_t = Σ_{k=0}^∞ γ^k R_{t+k+1}. Value functions quantify how good it is to be in a state (V^π(s)) or to take an action in a state (Q^π(s,a)) under a given policy. The Bellman equations relate these values recursively, enabling dynamic programming, Monte Carlo, and temporal‑difference methods to estimate them. An optimal policy π* satisfies the Bellman optimality equations, and algorithms such as value iteration, policy iteration, Q‑learning, and actor‑critic methods converge to π* under appropriate conditions. Understanding the MDP framework, reward shaping, and the distinction between on‑policy and off‑policy learning is essential before diving into deep RL architectures. Practitioners often start with tabular methods to grasp the theory before scaling to function approximation with neural networks.

---

## ✅ Key Points

- MDP defines states, actions, transition dynamics, rewards, and discount factor.
- Policy π maps states to action probabilities; the objective is to maximize expected discounted return.
- Value functions V^π and Q^π satisfy Bellman equations, enabling iterative solutions.
- Optimal policy satisfies Bellman optimality; algorithms like value iteration, Q‑learning converge to it.
- Distinguish on‑policy vs off‑policy learning and the role of reward shaping.

## 💡 Real-World Analogy

Training a dog with treats: the dog (agent) observes the situation (state), chooses a trick (action), receives a treat (reward), and learns a strategy (policy) to maximize future treats.

---

## ⚠️ Common Mistakes

- Treating the reward as immediate only, ignoring long‑term return.
- Assuming the environment is fully observable when it’s partially observable.
- Using a deterministic policy too early, preventing exploration.
